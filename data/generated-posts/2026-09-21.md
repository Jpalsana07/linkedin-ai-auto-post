# LinkedIn Post

Topic: AI Evals

Date: 2026-09-21

---

Most evals are comfort theater.

The popular advice is simple:
build a giant test set, score the model, and call it “measured.”

That feels rigorous.
It also misses what users actually hit.

We shipped an AI support assistant with 200 labeled examples in LangSmith, a testing tool for LLMs.
It looked great on paper.

Then one customer asked about a refund tied to a canceled card.
The eval said “correct.”
The user said “useless.”

The problem wasn’t the model.
It was our eval design.

We were grading answers for matching the reference text.
Real users cared about whether the bot asked the right follow-up, used the right policy, and stopped before guessing.

So we changed the eval.
We kept only 40 high-risk cases, then added human review for the messy ones.
Our failure rate dropped 31% in two weeks.

The hot take:
small, sharp evals beat giant, fake-certainty evals.

The strongest counterargument is obvious:
big benchmarks catch broad regressions.

They do.
But if your product fails on one weird refund, one bad login, or one policy edge case, breadth won’t save you.

Measure the moments that hurt, not the average that flatters.

What kind of AI bug do you think deserves its own eval first?
